\section{The 10-Step Process: From Blank Page to Standing Ovation}

\pullquote{Most presenters start by opening PowerPoint. That is like an architect starting by choosing doorknobs. The structure must exist before the surface does.}{Storymakers Method}

\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-10steps.jpg}{The 10-step process from blank page to standing ovation: Phase 0 (Launchpad), Phase 1 (Block level), Phase 2 (Loop level), and Phase 3 (Slide level).}

\subsection{The architecture of a presentation}

Every presentation that works---every pitch that closes, every keynote that resonates, every board deck that shifts opinion---follows the same underlying structure. Not the same story. Not the same slides. The same \emph{architecture}.

The Storymakers Method encodes that architecture into a 10-step process organised across four phases. The phases are not arbitrary. They map to the way human cognition processes persuasive communication: first the frame, then the argument, then the rhythm, then the signal.\endnote{Minto, Barbara, \emph{The Minto Pyramid Principle: Logic in Writing, Thinking, and Problem Solving}, Minto International, 1987. Minto's work at McKinsey established the foundational principle that structured communication begins with the answer, not the data.}

\begin{thesisbox}[The 10-Step Architecture]
\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, labelindent=0em]
  \item[Phase 0 — Launchpad] (Step 0): SCQA, Big Idea, Pillars
  \item[Phase 1 — Block Level] (Steps 1--3): Structure, Journey, Evidence
  \item[Phase 2 — Loop Level] (Steps 4--6): Sequence, Tension, Storyboard
  \item[Phase 3 — Slide Level] (Steps 7--9): Insight, Concept, Signal
\end{description}
\end{thesisbox}

The numbering begins at zero deliberately. Phase 0 is the most important phase in the process. It is also the one that most people skip entirely.

\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-time-allocation.jpg}{The 60/25/15 split that most presenters invert: 60\% on foundation and blocks, 25\% on loops, 15\% on slides. Swap these ratios and you are decorating a house with no walls.}

Before walking through each phase, a number that will feel counterintuitive: \textbf{60\% of total effort should go into Phase 0 and Phase 1.} The foundation and block-level structure. Not the slides. Not the visuals. Not the animations.

The result is a beautifully formatted argument that nobody follows, because the argument was never properly constructed.\endnote{Duarte, Nancy, \emph{Slide:ology: The Art and Science of Creating Great Presentations}, O'Reilly Media, 2008. \begin{alertbox}[THE INVERSION TRAP]
If you are spending more time choosing fonts than choosing your argument structure, you are building a house by decorating the living room before pouring the foundation. No amount of visual polish rescues a presentation whose core argument is unclear.
\end{alertbox}

\subsection{Phase 0 --- The Launchpad (Step 0)}

Phase 0 is a single step, but it consumes 20--30\% of total project time. It answers three questions that every subsequent step depends on:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{What is the situation, complication, question, and answer?} (SCQA)
  \item \textbf{What is the Big Idea?} (One sentence that captures the entire presentation)
  \item \textbf{What are the Pillars?} (The 2--4 MECE arguments that support the Big Idea)
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{SCQA: The strategic frame}

The SCQA framework---Situation, Complication, Question, Answer---is the argument engine of the Launchpad. See Chapter 4 for the full SCQA framework, test questions, and worked examples. In the 10-step process, the SCQA serves as the filter: every slide must connect back to this frame, or it does not belong.

\subsubsection{The Big Idea}

The Big Idea is a single sentence --- under 20 words --- that states what is at stake and contains your point of view (see Chapter 4 for the full Big Idea framework, Duarte's formula, and worked examples).\endnote{Duarte, Nancy, \emph{Resonate: Present Visual Stories That Transform Audiences}, Wiley, 2010.} A Big Idea demands a response; a topic demands nothing.

\subsubsection{Pillars: The MECE argument}

Pillars are the 2--4 top-level arguments that hold the Big Idea up. They must be MECE: Mutually Exclusive (no overlap between pillars) and Collectively Exhaustive (together they cover the full argument). See Chapter 6 for the Architect's full MECE toolkit, including diagnostic examples and the MECE test at every pyramid level. The Pillar Test: if you remove one pillar and the argument does not collapse, it is decoration---merge it or cut it.

\subsection{Phase 1 --- Block Level (Steps 1--3)}

Phase 1 introduces the Creative Trio: three perspectives that collaborate to build the presentation's block-level structure. Each perspective asks a different question about the same material.\endnote{The Creative Trio framework draws on cross-functional design thinking principles documented in Brown, Tim, \emph{Change by Design: How Design Thinking Transforms Organizations and Inspires Innovation}, Harper Business, 2009.}

\subsubsection{Step 1: The Architect --- Structure the Problem}

The Architect asks: \emph{Is the argument logically complete?}

This step takes the SCQA and Pillars from Phase 0 and builds them into Blocks---the major structural sections of the presentation. A typical presentation has 3--5 Blocks. Each Block maps to a Pillar or a necessary structural element (introduction, conclusion, call to action).

The Architect checks for MECE compliance: do the Blocks overlap? Are there gaps? Is there a Block that exists because the presenter likes the topic, rather than because the argument requires it?

\begin{diagnosisbox}
\textbf{The Architect's Diagnostic.} Take your Block outline and show it to someone who knows nothing about the topic. Can they follow the argument from Block titles alone? If they cannot, the structure is broken. No amount of content will fix a structural problem.
\end{diagnosisbox}

\subsubsection{Step 2: The Storyteller --- Define the Journey}

The Storyteller asks: \emph{Does the audience feel something?}

This step maps the emotional arc of the presentation. Where does tension rise? Where does it resolve? Where does the audience feel the weight of the problem before seeing the solution?\endnote{Zak, Paul J., ``Why Your Brain Loves Good Storytelling,'' \emph{Harvard Business Review}, October 2014. Zak's research demonstrated that narrative tension triggers oxytocin release, which increases empathy and willingness to cooperate---directly relevant to persuasive presentations.}

The Storyteller is not decorating the Architect's structure with anecdotes. The Storyteller is ensuring that the argument has a \emph{shape}---a rise and fall of energy that keeps attention alive. A presentation with perfect logic but no emotional arc will be admired and forgotten. A presentation with emotional resonance and solid logic will be remembered and acted upon.

\subsubsection{Step 3: The Designer --- Plan the Evidence}

The Designer asks: \emph{What will they see?}

This step plans the evidence strategy for each Block: which data, which examples, which visuals will make the argument tangible. The Designer does not open PowerPoint. The Designer decides what type of evidence each argument needs: a number, a chart, a case study, a comparison, a metaphor, or a physical demonstration.\endnote{Reynolds, Garr, \emph{Presentation Zen: Simple Ideas on Presentation Design and Delivery}, New Riders, 2008. Reynolds' work established the principle that evidence selection---deciding \emph{what} to show---precedes and outranks visual design---deciding \emph{how} to show it.}

\begin{keybox}[THE CREATIVE TRIO IN PHASE 1]
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item \textbf{Architect}: ``The argument requires these 4 Blocks in this order.''
  \item \textbf{Storyteller}: ``The tension peaks in Block 2. Block 3 provides resolution.''
  \item \textbf{Designer}: ``Block 1 needs a data chart. Block 2 needs a case study. Block 4 needs a single number.''
\end{itemize}
The three perspectives are sequential within Phase 1 but iterative across phases. A discovery by the Designer often forces the Architect to restructure.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The War Room: Where Ideas Become Structure}
\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-war-room.jpg}{The War Room: where Disney met McKinsey. Post-its on a whiteboard, Big Idea at the top, arguments clustering below. Get the ideas out of your head and onto the wall.}
The bridge between abstract strategy and tangible structure is storyboarding --- a technique borrowed from the film industry, where Walt Disney's team pioneered the practice of planning every scene visually before committing to animation.\endnote{The storyboarding technique was developed at Walt Disney Studios in the early 1930s. Disney artists pinned drawings to boards to visualise the flow of a story before animating, saving enormous time and cost.}

\begin{keybox}[THE WAR ROOM METHOD]
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Post your Big Idea and SCQA} on red post-its at the top of a whiteboard. This is your north star.
  \item \textbf{Silent brainstorm:} Every team member writes one idea per post-it --- a data point, an argument, a question, a potential slide title. No discussion yet. This prevents groupthink.
  \item \textbf{Cluster and organise (MECE):} Together, group related post-its. Name each group with a header post-it. Ensure the groups are MECE.
  \item \textbf{Sequence the story:} Arrange the groups in a logical narrative sequence. Which chapter comes first? What builds on what?
  \item \textbf{Create the storyboard:} Draw larger boxes on the board for each slide. Write a headline (action title) and sketch the visual. This is your rolling pack.
\end{enumerate}
This method is powerful because it is fast, flexible, and visual. You can move slides, rewrite headlines, and restructure the story in real time.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{The Rolling Pack: Your Living Document}
\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-rolling-pack.jpg}{The Rolling Pack: share the ugly draft early. Low-fidelity slides with action titles and placeholder charts. Not polished. Not pretty. But complete enough for feedback.}
The output of the storyboarding session is not a finished presentation. It is a \textbf{rolling pack} --- a low-fidelity PowerPoint or document that contains the complete structure but none of the polish. Every slide has a clear action title; in place of finished charts, there are descriptions: ``[CHART: Q1 vs Q2 sales comparison by region].''

\begin{alertbox}[THE FEEDBACK TRAP]
A team of junior consultants spent two weeks building an 80-slide presentation with complex analysis and polished charts. When the senior partner reviewed it, she said: ``The analysis is impressive, but the story is wrong. We're not answering the client's key question.'' The team had to start over. If they had shared a 10--15 slide rolling pack in the first three days, they would have received that feedback in time and saved two weeks of wasted work.\endnote{Composite case drawn from consulting practice. The rolling pack is standard methodology at McKinsey, BCG, and Bain for exactly this reason.}
\end{alertbox}

\subsection{Phase 2 --- Loop Level (Steps 4--6)}

If Blocks are chapters, Loops are paragraphs. A Loop is a self-contained argumentative unit within a Block: a single claim supported by evidence and followed by a transition. A typical Block contains 3--7 Loops.\endnote{The Loop concept as a unit of argumentation draws on Toulmin, Stephen E., \emph{The Uses of Argument}, Cambridge University Press, 1958. Toulmin's model of claim-data-warrant maps directly to the Loop's structure of assertion-evidence-transition.}

\subsubsection{Step 4: Sequence the Argument}

Each Loop within a Block must follow a logical sequence. The most common sequences are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Problem $\rightarrow$ Cause $\rightarrow$ Solution} (diagnostic narrative)
  \item \textbf{Claim $\rightarrow$ Evidence $\rightarrow$ Implication} (analytical narrative)
  \item \textbf{Before $\rightarrow$ After $\rightarrow$ How} (transformation narrative)
  \item \textbf{General $\rightarrow$ Specific $\rightarrow$ General} (inductive/deductive hybrid)
\end{itemize}

The choice of sequence is not aesthetic. It depends on the audience's starting position. An audience that already agrees with the problem needs a solution-first sequence. An audience that doubts the problem exists needs a diagnostic sequence.\endnote{Cialdini, Robert B., \emph{Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion}, revised edition, Harper Business, 2006. Cialdini's framework of pre-suasion---shaping the audience's mental frame before presenting the argument---directly informs Loop sequencing strategy.}

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} For every Block in your outline, list the Loops inside it and ask: \emph{why does Loop 2 follow Loop 1?} If the answer is ``because that is the order I thought of them,'' you have no sequence---you have a list. A genuine sequence means each Loop either builds on the previous one (deductive) or each Loop presents a parallel case that accumulates weight (inductive). Apply the Architect's MECE test from Chapter~6 to verify that the Loops within each Block are collectively exhaustive and mutually exclusive.

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 4.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Data-dump sequencing:} Ordering Loops by the order you found the data, rather than the order the audience needs to receive it.
  \item \textbf{Burying the lead:} Placing the most compelling evidence in the last Loop of a Block, where fatigued attention misses it. For sceptical audiences, lead with your strongest proof.
  \item \textbf{Missing transitions:} Two Loops may be individually strong but have no logical bridge between them. Every Loop must end with a sentence that makes the next Loop inevitable.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 4 QUALITY GATE]
Before proceeding to Step 5, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Every Block contains 3--7 Loops, each with an action title.
  \item The sequence type is identified for each Block (diagnostic, analytical, transformation, or hybrid).
  \item Reading only the Loop action titles within a Block produces a coherent mini-argument.
  \item Every Loop ends with a transition sentence that connects to the next Loop.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection{Step 5: Manage Tension}

Tension is the engine of attention. A presentation without tension is a lecture. The Storyteller returns in this step to map the tension profile across Loops within each Block. See Chapter~7 for the Storyteller's full tension-management toolkit, including emotional arc templates and pacing techniques.

\begin{insightbox}[The Tension Principle]
Every Loop should contain a moment where the audience does not yet know the answer. This gap between question and resolution is what holds attention. If you reveal the answer before the audience feels the question, you have killed the tension. If you sustain the question too long, you have lost the audience. The window is narrow: 30 to 90 seconds of sustained uncertainty per Loop.
\end{insightbox}

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} For each Block, draw a simple tension graph: the x-axis is time, the y-axis is audience uncertainty. Mark where each Loop opens a question and where it closes it. The graph should look like a sawtooth wave---repeated rises and falls---not a flat line (no tension) or a single peak (one big reveal buried in a monotone).

A well-managed Block opens with a \emph{tension beat}---a question, a surprising statistic, a contradiction---and resolves it within the Loop. Then the next Loop opens a new tension beat at a slightly higher emotional register than the last resolution. This ratcheting effect is what keeps audiences leaning forward.

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 5.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Answer-first syndrome:} Stating the conclusion before the audience has felt the problem. Tension requires a gap between question and answer---do not close it prematurely.
  \item \textbf{Tension without resolution:} Raising alarming questions and never answering them. This creates anxiety, not engagement.
  \item \textbf{Monotone energy:} Every Loop at the same emotional pitch. Variation is essential. A quiet, reflective Loop after a high-energy one resets the audience's attention capacity.
  \item \textbf{Ignoring the Storyteller's arc:} Tension management is not decoration on top of logic---it is a structural element. Revisit the Storyteller's emotional arc from Step~2 and ensure it is reflected at the Loop level.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 5 QUALITY GATE]
Before proceeding to Step 6, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Every Block contains at least two tension/release beats.
  \item No Loop resolves its question in the first sentence (too fast) or holds it for more than 90 seconds (too slow).
  \item The tension profile across the full presentation follows the emotional arc defined in Step 2.
  \item At least one ``surprise moment'' exists in the presentation---a data point, comparison, or reframe that the audience does not expect.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection{Step 6: Storyboard the Data}

Every chart, every number, every data visualisation in the presentation is planned in this step---not as a finished visual, but as a specification. The Designer returns to assign each Loop its evidence format. See Chapter~8 for the Designer's full evidence-selection framework and chart-type decision tree.\endnote{Knaflic, Cole Nussbaumer, \emph{Storytelling with Data: A Data Visualization Guide for Business Professionals}, Wiley, 2015. Knaflic's framework for choosing chart types based on the \emph{relationship} you want to show (comparison, composition, distribution, relationship) is the standard reference for Step 6.}

The storyboard answers: What data supports this Loop? What chart type communicates the insight? What is the ``so what?'' of this chart? If you cannot state the ``so what'' in one sentence, the chart does not belong.

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} For each Loop, write a one-line specification in the format: \texttt{[CHART TYPE: what it shows | SO WHAT: the insight]}. For example: \texttt{[LINE CHART: monthly churn rate Jan--Jun | SO WHAT: churn accelerated after the pricing change in March]}. This specification is what goes into the rolling pack. The actual chart is built later, in Phase~3.

The chart type must match the relationship you want to show:

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 6.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Chart without a ``so what'':} A chart that shows data but does not make a point. Every chart must have an action title that states the insight, not a topic label that describes the data.
  \item \textbf{Too many data series:} A chart with six lines or twelve bar groups obscures the signal. Simplify: show the comparison that matters, move the rest to an appendix.
  \item \textbf{Defaulting to tables:} Tables are reference material, not persuasion tools. If you are presenting live, replace tables with charts. If the audience needs the raw numbers, provide a leave-behind document.
  \item \textbf{Skipping the storyboard:} Going straight from Loop outline to finished slides. This results in charts that are built to show available data, not charts that are built to prove the argument.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 6 QUALITY GATE]
Before proceeding to Phase 3, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Every Loop has a data specification with a chart type and a ``so what'' sentence.
  \item No chart type is chosen arbitrarily---each matches the relationship being shown.
  \item The rolling pack is complete: every slide has an action title and a visual specification.
  \item Reading the rolling pack end-to-end tells the full story, even without finished visuals.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Phase 3 --- Slide Level (Steps 7--9)}

Phase 3 is where PowerPoint (or Keynote, or Google Slides) finally opens. Not before. Only after 85\% of the intellectual work is complete.

\subsubsection{Step 7: Sharpen the Insight}

Every slide gets an \textbf{action title}---a complete sentence that states the slide's argument, not a topic label. See Chapter~6 for the Architect's full treatment of action titles versus topic titles, and Chapter~5 for the Headline Test. The core principle: if you read only the action titles from first slide to last, the entire argument should be clear without seeing any slide body.

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} Take the rolling pack from Step 6 and rewrite every placeholder title as a full action sentence. This is the single highest-leverage edit in the entire process. A title like ``Q2 Churn Data'' becomes ``Churn accelerated to 14\% in Q2, driven by first-90-day attrition.'' The difference is not stylistic---it is structural. The action title tells the audience what to conclude from the slide before they process the visual. It pre-loads their cognitive frame.

The \textbf{Headline Test}: print all action titles on a single page, without any slide bodies. Read them top to bottom. If the full argument is clear, your titles are strong. If there are gaps, missing logic, or vague statements, those slides need sharper titles.

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 7.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Topic titles disguised as action titles:} ``Customer churn analysis'' is a topic. ``Customer churn tripled among users who skipped onboarding'' is an action title. The test: does the title contain a verb and a point of view?
  \item \textbf{Titles that contradict the visual:} If the action title says ``Revenue grew steadily'' but the chart shows a plateau, the audience loses trust. Titles and visuals must be perfectly aligned.
  \item \textbf{Titles that are too long:} An action title should be one sentence, typically 8--15 words. If it requires two sentences, the slide is trying to make two points---split it.
  \item \textbf{Skipping the Headline Test:} This is the single most reliable diagnostic for presentation quality. Do not skip it.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 7 QUALITY GATE]
Before proceeding to Step 8, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Every slide has an action title (a full sentence with a verb and a point of view).
  \item The Headline Test passes: reading all titles in sequence tells the complete argument.
  \item No title exceeds 15 words.
  \item No two adjacent titles make the same point (redundancy check).
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection{Step 8: Visualise the Concept}

Each slide's body must reinforce its action title with a single visual concept: a chart, a diagram, a photograph, a comparison, or a single number rendered large. The rule is one concept per slide.\endnote{Reynolds, Garr, \emph{Presentation Zen}, op.\ cit. Reynolds' ``one idea per slide'' principle draws on cognitive load theory: working memory can hold 4$\pm$1 chunks simultaneously (Cowan, Nelson, ``The Magical Number 4 in Short-Term Memory,'' \emph{Behavioral and Brain Sciences}, 2001).}

\begin{alertbox}[THE COGNITIVE LIMIT]
Working memory holds approximately four items simultaneously. A slide with six bullet points, a chart, and a footnote is asking the audience to process ten concepts at once. The result is not comprehension. It is noise. One concept per slide is not minimalism---it is cognitive science.
\end{alertbox}

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} For each slide, choose the single visual format that best serves the action title. This is a design decision, not a formatting decision. Ask: \emph{what visual will make this argument undeniable in three seconds?} Sometimes the answer is a chart. Sometimes it is a single number rendered at 120-point font. Sometimes it is a before/after comparison. Sometimes it is a photograph that triggers an emotional response.

The visual hierarchy on the slide must match the intellectual hierarchy. The most important element should be the largest, the most central, and the highest-contrast. Secondary elements (source labels, footnotes, axis labels) should recede visually. See Chapter~8 for the Designer's full visual hierarchy framework.

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 8.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two concepts on one slide:} A bar chart and a table on the same slide forces the audience to choose what to look at. Split into two slides.
  \item \textbf{Decoration masquerading as concept:} Stock photography, gradient backgrounds, and clip art are not visual concepts. They are filler. If the visual does not reinforce the action title, remove it.
  \item \textbf{Chart as default:} Not every slide needs a chart. Some of the most powerful slides contain a single sentence, a single number, or a single image. Match the format to the argument.
  \item \textbf{Ignoring the ``three-second test'':} Show the slide to someone for three seconds. If they cannot identify the main point, the visual hierarchy is wrong.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 8 QUALITY GATE]
Before proceeding to Step 9, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item Every slide contains exactly one visual concept.
  \item The visual reinforces (not merely accompanies) the action title.
  \item The three-second test passes: a stranger can identify the main point in three seconds.
  \item No slide contains decorative elements that do not serve the argument.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\subsubsection{Step 9: Refine the Signal}

The final step removes everything that does not serve the argument. Every word, every colour, every line on a chart must justify its existence. This is where the Designer becomes an editor: cutting decoration, reducing labels, eliminating redundancy, and ensuring that the visual hierarchy on each slide matches the intellectual hierarchy of the argument.

Signal-to-noise ratio is the metric. A slide with 80\% signal and 20\% noise is good. A slide with 50\% signal and 50\% noise is mediocre. Most corporate slides are below 30\% signal.\endnote{Tufte, Edward R., \emph{The Visual Display of Quantitative Information}, 2nd edition, Graphics Press, 2001. Tufte's concept of ``data-ink ratio''---the proportion of a chart's ink devoted to displaying data versus non-data elements---is the quantitative foundation for the signal-to-noise principle.}

\paragraph{What it means in practice.} Apply the \textbf{Squint Test} to every slide: squint until the content blurs. What you see first is what the audience processes first. If the first thing you see is a logo, a footnote, or a decorative border, the visual hierarchy is wrong. The first thing you see should be the main point of the slide.

Then apply \textbf{progressive elimination}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Remove all gridlines from charts. Add them back only if the audience needs to read precise values.
  \item Remove all axis labels that the action title already conveys.
  \item Remove all legends that can be replaced by direct labels on the data.
  \item Remove all colours except those that carry meaning. Grey is the default; colour is reserved for emphasis.
  \item Remove all text below 14-point font. If it is too small to read in a presentation, it does not belong on the slide.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Common mistakes at Step 9.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Confusing polish with signal:} Adding drop shadows, gradients, and 3D effects increases visual complexity without increasing information. Polish is reduction, not addition.
  \item \textbf{Source-line clutter:} Twelve source footnotes on a single slide. Move sources to an appendix or a leave-behind document.
  \item \textbf{Inconsistent formatting:} Different fonts, colours, or chart styles across slides. Inconsistency forces the audience to re-learn the visual language on every slide, consuming cognitive resources that should go to the argument.
  \item \textbf{Refusing to cut:} Every presenter has a favourite slide that took hours to build but does not serve the argument. Cut it. Move it to the appendix. The presentation is not a museum of your effort---it is a vehicle for your argument.
\end{itemize}

\begin{keybox}[STEP 9 QUALITY GATE]
Before declaring the presentation complete, verify:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em]
  \item The Squint Test passes on every slide: the first thing visible is the main point.
  \item No slide contains more than 30 words (excluding the action title).
  \item All charts have been simplified: no unnecessary gridlines, legends, or axis labels.
  \item Colour is used for emphasis only---no decorative colour.
  \item The Headline Test still passes after all visual refinements.
\end{itemize}
\end{keybox}

\caption{Quality gates between phases. Skipping a gate is the most common source of presentation failure.}
\label{tab:quality-gates}
\end{table}

\begin{insightbox}[Why Gates Matter]
The cost of fixing a structural problem increases exponentially as you move through phases. A Pillar change in Phase 0 costs 30 minutes. The same change discovered in Phase 3---after slides are built---costs 3 hours. Quality gates exist to catch structural flaws early, when they are cheap to fix.
\end{insightbox}

% ============================================================================
\subsection{The process is iterative, not linear}

The 10 steps are numbered for clarity, not because they execute in strict sequence. Every practitioner of the method will revisit earlier steps as new insights emerge in later steps. This is not failure. It is the process working correctly.

Common iteration patterns:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Step 6 $\rightarrow$ Step 1}: Storyboarding data reveals that a Block lacks sufficient evidence. The Architect restructures.
  \item \textbf{Step 7 $\rightarrow$ Step 2}: Writing action titles reveals that the emotional arc is flat. The Storyteller remaps.
  \item \textbf{Step 8 $\rightarrow$ Step 0}: Visualising the concept exposes a weakness in the Big Idea itself. Back to the Launchpad.
  \item \textbf{Step 5 $\rightarrow$ Step 4}: Managing tension within a Loop reveals that the Loop sequence is wrong. Reorder.
\end{itemize}

\begin{diagnosisbox}
\textbf{The Iteration Signal.} If you never revisit an earlier step, you are either a genius or you are not looking hard enough. In practice, teams iterate 2--3 times across phases before converging. The process is a spiral, not a waterfall. Each iteration tightens the argument, sharpens the story, and clarifies the evidence.
\end{diagnosisbox}

\pullquote{A presentation is not built in ten steps. It is built in ten steps, revisited three times, with the courage to kill ideas that felt brilliant at Step 2 but collapse at Step 7.}{Storymakers Method}

\subsection{A Complete Walkthrough: Building a Board Presentation in 10 Steps}
\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-scqa-to-slides.jpg}{From SCQA to slides in 10 steps: the thinking becomes the deck. Abstract strategy transforms into concrete deliverable through four phases.}
The following walkthrough applies the entire 10-step process to a single scenario from start to finish. Every step produces concrete output. Follow along with your own scenario, and by the end you will have a complete presentation architecture.

\begin{thesisbox}[The Scenario]
You are VP of Marketing at a SaaS company with \texteuro{}50M in annual recurring revenue. Customer churn has increased from 8\% to 14\% over the past six months. You need to present to the board of directors to secure approval for a \texteuro{}500K investment in building a dedicated customer success team. The board is risk-averse, financially oriented, and time-poor. You have 20 minutes.
\end{thesisbox}

% --- Step 0 ---
\subsubsection*{Step 0 --- The Launchpad: Define the Strategic Frame}

Apply the SCQA framework from Chapter~4 to establish the argument's foundation.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\rowcolors{2}{altrow}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries\color{heading}}l X}
\toprule
\rowcolor{tableheader}
\textcolor{white}{\textbf{SCQA Element}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Content}} \\
\midrule
Situation & Our SaaS platform grew 40\% last year, adding 1,200 new customers and reaching \texteuro{}50M ARR. \\
Complication & Churn jumped from 8\% to 14\% in six months---we are now losing customers faster than we gain them. At this rate, net growth stalls within two quarters. \\
Question & How do we stop the bleeding before growth stalls entirely? \\
Answer & Invest \texteuro{}500K in a dedicated customer success team. The investment pays for itself in 8 months from retained revenue alone. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{SCQA for the board presentation. Note how the Answer contains both the recommendation and its financial justification---essential for a financially oriented audience.}
\end{table}

\noindent\textbf{Big Idea:} ``Every 1\% of churn we prevent is worth \texteuro{}420K in annual recurring revenue---the customer success team pays for itself in 8 months.''

\noindent\textbf{Three Pillars (MECE):}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The churn problem is concentrated in the first 90 days after onboarding.
  \item Competitors with customer success teams retain 3$\times$ better in this segment.
  \item The investment pays back in 8 months with a conservative retention improvement of 3 percentage points.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Audience profile:} Board of directors. Risk-averse: they will look for reasons to say no. Financially oriented: every claim must have a number. Time-poor: 20 minutes maximum, no tangents.

\begin{insightbox}[Gate 1 Check]
\textbf{Is the Big Idea something they would repeat to a colleague?} Test: imagine a board member leaving the meeting and running into the CFO in the hallway. Would they say ``Every 1\% of churn we prevent is worth \texteuro{}420K---the CS team pays back in 8 months''? If yes, the Big Idea passes. If the sentence is too complex or too vague to relay in a hallway, rewrite it.
\end{insightbox}

\subsubsection*{Step 1 --- The Architect: Structure the Problem}

The Architect transforms the three Pillars into Blocks. Apply the Architect's MECE test from Chapter~6 to verify no overlap and no gaps.

\toprule
\rowcolor{tableheader}
\textcolor{white}{\textbf{Block}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Title}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Argument}} \\
\midrule
1 & The Churn Crisis & Churn doubled in 6 months. 68\% of lost customers leave in the first 90 days. The problem is concentrated and diagnosable. \\
2 & The Competitive Gap & Our top 3 competitors all have CS teams. Their 90-day retention is 91\% vs.\ our 74\%. We are losing to a solved problem. \\
3 & The Investment Case & A 5-person CS team costs \texteuro{}500K/year. Retaining just 3\% more customers adds \texteuro{}1.26M in ARR. Payback: 8 months. \\

\noindent\textbf{MECE validation:} Does removing any Block collapse the argument? Without Block~1, there is no urgency. Without Block~2, there is no proof that the solution works. Without Block~3, there is no financial case. All three are load-bearing.

The Storyteller maps the emotional arc across Blocks. For the full emotional arc toolkit, see Chapter~7.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\rowcolors{2}{altrow}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries\color{heading}}c l l X}
\toprule
\rowcolor{tableheader}
\textcolor{white}{\textbf{Block}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Opens with}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Emotion}} & \textcolor{white}{\textbf{Closes with}} \\

Never reverse this order---presenting the solution before the audience feels the problem removes all tension.

% --- Step 9 ---
\subsubsection*{Step 9 --- Refine the Signal: The Squint Test}

Apply the Squint Test to the churn trend chart (Slide 1.1). Before refinement, the chart has: six gridlines, an x-axis with monthly labels, a y-axis with percentage labels, a legend box, a source footnote, a subtitle, and a decorative gradient background.

\noindent\textbf{Progressive elimination:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Remove the gradient background. (It adds zero information.)
  \item Remove all but two gridlines (8\% baseline and 14\% current). (Only these two reference points matter.)
  \item Remove the legend. (There is only one data series---label it directly.)
  \item Shrink the source footnote to 8-point font and move it to the bottom-left corner.
  \item Add a red annotation circle around the inflection point where churn began accelerating.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent After refinement: the audience's eye goes immediately to the red circle at the inflection point, then follows the line up to 14\%. The action title confirms what they see. Total processing time: under five seconds.

\begin{alertbox}[THE FINAL CHECK]
Before presenting, run four tests in sequence:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The Headline Test:} Read all action titles end-to-end. Does the argument hold?
  \item \textbf{The Squint Test:} Squint at every slide. Is the main point the first thing you see?
  \item \textbf{The ``So What'' Test:} Point to any chart and ask ``So what?'' Can you answer in one sentence?
  \item \textbf{The Hallway Test:} Can a board member summarise your argument to the CFO in 30 seconds?
\end{enumerate}
If all four pass, your presentation is ready.
\end{alertbox}

\begin{insightbox}[Gate 4 Check]
\textbf{Does the Squint Test pass on every slide? Is there one idea per slide?} This is the final quality gate. Walk through every slide in presentation mode (full screen). If any slide requires more than five seconds of processing to identify the main point, it fails. Simplify until it passes.
\end{insightbox}

\subsection{The Four Quality Gates: A Summary}
\sourcefig{infographics/ch09-quality-gates.jpg}{The four quality gates: checkpoints between phases that prevent bad structure from infecting the next level. Don't proceed until you pass.}
The quality gates are not optional checkpoints---they are structural requirements. Skipping a gate does not save time; it moves the cost of fixing structural problems to a later phase, where the cost is 3--10$\times$ higher.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
